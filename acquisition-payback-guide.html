How to calculate acquisition payback after replacing the owner

A cheaper listing is not necessarily a better purchase. Compare the cash flow that would remain after someone else performs the seller’s work.

1. Convert owner workload into a real cost

Request a representative task log, not just an estimate of weekly hours. Separate support, maintenance, marketing, bookkeeping and specialist work. Obtain replacement quotes and add overhead. Count only incremental expenses, not expenses already deducted in reported profit.

Estimate monthly replacement costs for free.

2. Recalculate profit

Adjusted monthly profit = reported monthly profit − incremental replacement cost − other incremental monthly costs. Check bank statements and reconcile revenue and expenses before trusting reported profit.

3. Stress-test the deal

For a simple profit shock, reduce positive reported profit by a chosen percentage, then subtract replacement and extra costs. Keep fixed costs unchanged in the example. This is a screening assumption, not a forecast of revenue or margins.

4. Compare the capital at risk

Simple payback = (purchase price + reserve and fees) ÷ positive adjusted monthly profit. If profit is zero or negative there is no positive payback. The formula does not incorporate financing, taxes, discount rates, future investment, or resale value.

5. Ask for evidence before making an offer

  • Monthly statements and a reconciliation to payment processor and bank records.
  • Owner time logs, contractor costs, and written procedures for recurring tasks.
  • Customer and traffic concentration, churn, seasonality and platform dependency.
  • Transferability of accounts, licenses, IP and key supplier agreements.
  • Working capital, debt, tax, security and transition requirements reviewed with appropriate advisers.

Record the source and uncertainty of each assumption. Obtain independent quotes before assuming the work can be outsourced.
